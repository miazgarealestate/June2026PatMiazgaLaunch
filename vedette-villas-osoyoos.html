What Strata Villa Ownership Means

When you purchase a unit at Vedette Villas, you hold title to your individual strata lot — typically the interior of your unit, including fixtures, flooring, and appliances. The title is registered in the Land Title Office in your name, the same as any other BC real property transaction. This is genuine ownership, not a rental or a leasehold arrangement.

The land under the complex and all shared areas — exterior walls, roofing, landscaping, parking, common hallways, and any shared amenities — are owned collectively by the strata corporation. Every owner holds a share of that corporation proportional to their unit entitlement. This is what allows the maintenance costs of shared infrastructure to be divided fairly across all owners.

Monthly strata fees cover the strata corporation's operating costs: building insurance, landscaping, snow removal, routine maintenance of common areas, and contributions to the contingency reserve fund. The reserve fund is the strata's savings account for large future expenditures — roof replacement, parking lot resurfacing, major mechanical work. A well-funded reserve is one of the most important things to verify before purchasing in any strata complex.

The strata also governs certain aspects of how you use your unit. Bylaws may address restrictions on pets, rentals, parking, alterations, and noise. As a 55+ community, Vedette Villas has age-restriction bylaws requiring that at least one resident per unit meet the minimum age requirement. Reviewing the bylaws before making an offer is essential, not optional.

What Buyers Should Review Before Making an Offer

Strata purchases carry a distinct due diligence checklist compared to freehold purchases. The documents listed below are your window into the financial health and governance of the corporation you are buying into. Request them early and read them carefully.

Strata Meeting Minutes

The minutes of annual general meetings and council meetings for the past two years tell you what issues have come up, how the strata has responded, and what problems may be on the horizon. Look for recurring maintenance concerns, owner disputes, or deferred work that has been acknowledged but not resolved. Minutes are the single most revealing document in any strata package.

Contingency Reserve Fund

The CRF balance tells you whether the strata has been setting money aside for major capital expenses. A low or depleted reserve relative to the age and condition of the complex is a warning sign: it means a special levy — a one-time charge to all owners — may be coming to fund repairs the reserve cannot cover. Ask for the most recent financial statements and compare the CRF balance to the depreciation report's projections.

Strata Bylaws

The bylaws govern what you can and cannot do in your unit and on strata property. For a 55+ community, the age restriction is the most important bylaw to understand fully, including any provisions for surviving spouses or co-owners who do not meet the age requirement. Bylaws also address pets, rentals, parking, and alterations — any of which may affect your plans for the property.

Depreciation Report

BC strata corporations with more than four lots are required to obtain a depreciation report every three years unless owners vote to waive it. The report is an independent assessment of the complex's major components — roofing, envelope, mechanical systems, paving — and projects replacement costs over 30 years. It is the tool that tells you whether the strata's current reserve fund contributions are adequate. If the report is old or has been waived repeatedly, factor that uncertainty into your offer.
