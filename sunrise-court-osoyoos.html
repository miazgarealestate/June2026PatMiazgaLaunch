Ownership Structure

What Strata Townhome Ownership Means at Sunrise Court

Sunrise Court is a strata townhome complex — a form of ownership governed by BC's Strata Property Act. When you purchase a unit here, you receive freehold title to your individual strata lot, which is the interior of your unit and, typically, the exclusive-use areas immediately surrounding it. You also become a member of the strata corporation and hold an undivided interest in the common property shared with other owners.

In practice, the strata corporation — made up of all unit owners — is responsible for the maintenance and repair of the building envelope (roofs, exterior walls, foundations), common areas (driveways, landscaping, any shared amenities), and the systems that serve the building as a whole. You are responsible for maintaining the interior of your own unit and any alterations you make to it, subject to the bylaws.

This division of responsibility is one of the practical advantages of strata living for the 55+ buyer. Major exterior maintenance — the roof, the exterior painting, the driveways — is handled collectively. You are not personally responsible for climbing a ladder or arranging a roofing contractor. The cost of that work is shared across all owners through monthly strata fees and, for larger projects, the contingency reserve fund.

The strata corporation is governed by a strata council elected by the owners. Council meetings and annual general meetings are the primary forums for community decisions. As an owner, you have a vote and a voice in how the community is managed. Reviewing the minutes of these meetings is one of the most useful things a prospective buyer can do — they reveal how the community functions, what issues have come up, and how the council has responded.

You Own and Maintain

Your individual strata unit — the interior space, fixtures, appliances, and any improvements you make. You hold freehold title registered at the BC Land Title Office. Interior maintenance, decorating, and improvements are your responsibility, subject to bylaw approval where required.

The Strata Maintains

Common property: the building envelope, roof, exterior, driveways, pathways, and shared landscaping. The strata corporation funds this maintenance through monthly fees and the contingency reserve fund. Major repairs are planned and voted on by owners.

Ground-Level Design

Sunrise Court's rancher-style units are built without interior stairs — a deliberate design choice for accessibility. Entry is at ground level, and all living spaces are on a single floor. For buyers who want the independence of a private home without the physical demands of a two-storey, this is a meaningful practical advantage.

The Osoyoos Advantage

55+ Living in Canada's Warmest City

Osoyoos sits at the southern tip of the Okanagan Valley in BC's only true desert — a semi-arid landscape of sagebrush, vineyards, and fruit orchards surrounding one of Canada's warmest freshwater lakes. For buyers entering retirement or semi-retirement, the climate alone is a compelling factor: long, hot summers, mild springs and autumns, and winters that rarely bring the snow and cold that characterize most of the rest of the country.

Osoyoos Lake is the centre of summer life in the community. Swimming, kayaking, paddleboarding, and fishing are minutes from Sunrise Court's front door. The town's waterfront beach and boardwalk are walkable. For wine enthusiasts, the Okanagan's southernmost wine region — Okanagan Falls to Oliver to Osoyoos — is home to some of Canada's most acclaimed estate wineries, and many of them are a short drive from town.

The town itself has the services buyers need without the congestion of larger centres: a hospital and medical clinic, grocery and pharmacy services, restaurants ranging from casual to destination dining, and a regional airport at Penticton for those travelling further. The international border crossing at Osoyoos into Washington State is minutes away, making cross-border shopping and the warmth of the Washington desert accessible for day trips.

For buyers weighing Osoyoos against other South Okanagan communities, the lake access and the climate are the two factors that consistently distinguish it. Oliver, 20 minutes north, offers many of the same amenities at a slightly lower price point, but without the lakefront. Buyers who want Osoyoos weather and Osoyoos water at a price that reflects their budget will find the 55+ strata market — communities like Sunrise Court — to be the most accessible entry point in the market.

Due Diligence

What to Review Before Removing Subjects

A strata purchase involves a set of documents that have no equivalent in a single-family freehold transaction. Understanding what you're buying means understanding the financial health and governance of the strata corporation — not just the condition of the individual unit. Your offer should be subject to your review and satisfaction with all of the following.

  • Strata Meeting Minutes (2–3 years): The minutes are the most informative document in the strata package. They record what issues have come up, what repairs have been done, what has been deferred, and any disputes or complaints within the community. Patterns of deferred maintenance or recurring complaints about the building envelope are early warning signs. Read the minutes carefully, particularly any mentions of upcoming assessments or capital projects.
  • Financial Statements: Review the most recent audited or unaudited financial statements. You want to understand whether the strata is operating within its budget, whether the reserve fund is being adequately funded, and whether there are any unusual expenses or deficits. A strata that consistently underfunds its reserve is accumulating a future liability that will eventually be passed on to owners as a special levy.
  • Contingency Reserve Fund: The reserve fund is the strata's savings account for major future expenses — roof replacement, exterior painting, repaving. The Form B Information Certificate provided by the seller will show the current balance. A balance that seems low relative to the age and condition of the buildings should prompt further investigation into whether a depreciation report exists and what it projects.
  • Special Levies: Ask specifically whether any special levies have been passed or are being discussed. A special levy is a one-time charge to all owners to cover a major expense. If one has been voted on and passed, you may be responsible for the remaining portion as the new owner. If one is being discussed but not yet passed, you should factor the potential cost into your offer decision.
  • Bylaws and Rules: The strata's bylaws govern what owners can and cannot do with their units. In a 55+ community, the age restriction bylaw is fundamental — confirm the exact language, including how it applies in situations where a resident passes away or a couple has one partner below age. Pet restrictions are also common in 55+ strata communities — if you have a pet, verify the pet policy in detail before committing. Rental restrictions determine whether you can rent your unit if your circumstances change.
  • Pet and Rental Restrictions: These two bylaw categories deserve particular attention at Sunrise Court and communities like it. Some 55+ stratas prohibit or strictly limit rentals to preserve the owner-occupied character of the community. Others have limits on the size or number of pets. Both restrictions can affect your flexibility and should be verified against your personal plans before removing subjects.
  • Home Inspection: A pre-purchase inspection of the individual unit is always recommended. For a strata townhome, the inspector should focus on the interior systems (plumbing, electrical, HVAC), the condition of the unit's own windows and doors, and the condition of any exclusive-use outdoor space. Note that the inspector cannot assess the common property — roof condition and exterior envelope are the strata's responsibility, so ask the strata council directly about any recent repairs or known issues with the building.
  • Depreciation Report: If the strata corporation has had a depreciation report prepared, reviewing it will give you the most complete picture of projected future expenses. The report identifies major common property components, estimates their remaining life, and projects replacement costs. It is the most useful tool available for assessing the adequacy of the reserve fund.
